GRIEF LIVES IN
THE BODY TOO

Understanding grief, the brain and the physical experience of loss

Grief can be difficult to make sense of.

You may understand intellectually that someone is gone, yet still find yourself expecting them to call. You might walk into a room and instinctively look for them. You might reach for your phone before remembering that you can no longer send them a message.

And then there are the physical experiences. The heaviness in your chest. The exhaustion. A tight throat. Difficulty sleeping. Changes in appetite. Restlessness. A feeling of being completely disconnected from yourself.

None of this means you are grieving incorrectly.

Grief is not simply an emotion that happens in your mind. It involves the brain, the body, memory, attachment and the process of adapting to a world that has changed.

There is no single way to grieve, and there is no timeline you need to follow.

What happens in your brain
when you grieve?

When we lose someone close to us, we don't simply lose a person. We lose an entire network of expectations built around that relationship.

Your brain has learned:

  • where that person usually is,
  • when you normally interact with them,
  • what they sound like,
  • what they mean to you,
  • and what it feels like to be connected to them.

After a loss, these expectations don't immediately disappear just because you understand that the person is gone.

Your mind can know something before your brain has fully adapted to it.

This can help explain experiences that are very common in grief: expecting someone's message, instinctively looking for them, or briefly forgetting that they are no longer there.

In his Huberman Lab episode on grief, neuroscientist Andrew Huberman discusses how neural circuits involved in emotional and factual memory interact with those involved in love and attachment, contributing to feelings of absence and yearning. He also describes the process of adapting our representations of a relationship, including its associations with space, time and emotional closeness.

This doesn't mean that grief is simply a problem of "rewiring your brain."

It means that the reality of the loss and your existing expectations have to gradually become integrated.

Why do you still expect
them to be there?

One of the strangest parts of losing someone can be knowing they're gone while still expecting them to be there.

You might instinctively reach for your phone. You might hear a familiar song and immediately think of them. You might walk into a place where they used to be and expect, for a split second, to see them.

These moments can feel confusing, but they make sense when we consider how relationships become embedded in everyday life. A close relationship isn't stored as a single memory. Over time, your brain builds a vast network of associations around that person: places, routines, conversations, memories and expectations.

When someone dies, those associations remain while the person is no longer physically present. Your conscious mind can understand: They're gone. While another part of your brain is still working from: They should be here.

Part of grieving involves gradually adapting those expectations to the new reality.

This may also help explain why grief can involve such intense yearning. You aren't simply remembering someone you miss. Your attachment system has learned that this person matters, expects connection with them and is now confronted with the fact that the relationship can no longer happen in the same physical way.

Research into the neuroscience of grief has found activity in brain regions involved in reward and motivation in people experiencing intense grief when they are shown reminders of the person who died. This offers one possible explanation for the powerful pull towards the person that can accompany bereavement.

Knowing someone is gone doesn't automatically switch off the systems in your brain that are built around loving and connecting with them.

Grief isn't a
five-step process

You may have heard of the five stages of grief:

DENIAL → ANGER → BARGAINING → DEPRESSION → ACCEPTANCE

The framework was introduced by psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in On Death and Dying, based on her work with people facing terminal illness. Years later, Kübler-Ross and grief expert David Kessler adapted the framework to bereavement in On Grief and Grieving.

The stages have become one of the best-known ways of talking about grief. And they can be useful as a language for describing experiences that may arise during grief. The important distinction is that they aren't intended to be a checklist.

You don't have to experience all five. You don't necessarily experience them in this order. You may move between them, experience several at once, or find that some don't resonate with you at all.

You might feel acceptance one day and anger the next. You might experience relief alongside sadness. You might feel relatively okay for several hours and then be overwhelmed by a memory.

None of these experiences mean you're grieving incorrectly.

And acceptance doesn't necessarily mean being okay with what happened. It can mean gradually recognising the reality of what has happened and learning to live within that new reality.

You don't have to stop loving someone to grieve

There is also an important shift in how we think about what it means to "move on." Moving forward doesn't necessarily mean leaving the relationship behind.

The concept of continuing bonds describes the ways people may maintain a meaningful connection with someone who has died. Through memories, traditions, stories, values, photographs or other personal rituals.

Research doesn't suggest that continuing bonds are always helpful or always harmful. A systematic review of 79 studies found that continuing bonds can be associated with both comfort and distress, as well as with questions around identity and how a person understands their relationship with the deceased.

So perhaps the question isn't: "How do I stop missing them?"

It might instead be: "How do I learn to live with this loss while allowing the relationship to remain meaningful?"

Grief isn't necessarily about letting go of the person.

It can be about learning how to carry the relationship differently.

Why can grief feel
so physical?

Grief doesn't stay neatly inside your thoughts. It can affect sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and your experience of your body.

You might notice:

  • heaviness or tightness in your chest
  • a lump or tightness in your throat
  • fatigue or low energy
  • changes in appetite
  • difficulty sleeping
  • headaches or other physical discomfort
  • restlessness
  • feeling numb or disconnected
  • difficulty concentrating

Research on bereavement supports an association between grief and physical health, particularly when grief becomes prolonged or severe. A 2025 systematic review of prolonged grief disorder found associations with insomnia, somatic symptom distress, blood pressure and other physical health outcomes, while also emphasising that more research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms.

The experience is different for everyone.

And I think an important distinction is that not every physical sensation during grief needs to be explained as "nervous-system dysregulation."

The nervous system is certainly involved in our response to stress and emotion, but grief is a much more complex experience than a single biological state.

Rather than deciding in advance what your body should be doing, it can be more useful to approach it with curiosity:

What am I actually noticing right now?

Grief and
everyday life

One influential framework for understanding grief is the Dual Process Model of Coping with Bereavement, developed by researchers Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut at Utrecht University.

Rather than describing grief as a sequence of stages, the model describes a movement between two broad orientations.

Loss-oriented

Turning towards the loss itself: remembering the person, feeling sadness, experiencing yearning and confronting the reality of what has happened.

Restoration-oriented

Turning towards life: managing practical responsibilities, developing new routines, spending time with other people and engaging with life outside the loss.

The important part is the movement between the two.

You might spend an afternoon crying about someone you have lost and then meet a friend for dinner that evening. You might spend several hours feeling relatively normal before suddenly being overwhelmed by a memory.

Both experiences can coexist. You don't have to be thinking about your loss every minute in order to be grieving.

Moments of stepping away from grief can also be part of grieving.

What can actually help
when you're grieving?

There is no exercise that can make grief disappear. And I would be cautious of anyone promising one. But there are ways of supporting yourself while you adapt to the reality of the loss.

1. Let grief and everyday life coexist

You don't have to choose between grieving and living. You can cry in the morning and laugh with a friend that evening. You can have a good day and still miss someone deeply.

You can take a break from thinking about the loss without abandoning the person you lost.

Try asking yourself:

Do I need some space for my grief right now, or do I need some space from my grief?

Either answer is okay.

2. Notice where grief shows up in your body

You don't need to change what you feel. Just notice.

Take a moment and ask yourself:

Where do I notice my grief in my body right now?

Maybe you notice:

  • A heaviness in your chest.
  • Tension in your throat.
  • A hollow feeling in your stomach.
  • Warmth around your face.
  • Restlessness in your legs.
  • Or perhaps you don't notice anything at all. That's okay too.

If you notice a sensation, stay with it for a few moments without trying to make it disappear.

You might ask:

  • Is it heavy or light?
  • Warm or cold?
  • Still or moving?
  • Does it change when I pay attention to it?

This isn't about "releasing grief from the body." It is simply an opportunity to practise noticing your experience without immediately needing to change it.

A simple practice you can try

If you're comfortable, take a moment to sit somewhere quiet.

Place your feet on the floor.

Look around the room and notice where you are.

Then bring your attention to your body. You don't need to change your breathing.

Simply notice:

What am I aware of right now?

Maybe there is sadness.

Maybe there is tension.

Maybe there is nothing obvious.

Then notice something that is supporting you in this moment: the chair beneath you, your feet on the floor, the temperature of the room or a familiar sound.

Spend a few moments moving your attention between what is difficult and what is supportive.

You aren't trying to get rid of the grief.

You're simply giving yourself permission to notice that more than one thing can exist at the same time.

You can miss someone and still feel your feet on the ground.

You can feel sadness and still notice warmth.

You can grieve and still be here.

3. Give yourself small moments of restoration

When you're grieving, everyday tasks can feel strangely difficult.

  • Cooking.
  • Going to work.
  • Answering messages.
  • Getting outside.
  • Seeing friends.

You don't need to do everything.

Start with the basics.

  • Eat something.
  • Take a shower.
  • Go for a short walk.
  • Call someone you trust.
  • Get outside for ten minutes.

These things aren't ways of "moving on." They are ways of continuing to take care of yourself while something painful is happening.

You are allowed to have moments where you don't think about the person you lost.

4. Make space for connection

Grief can be isolating. Sometimes other people don't know what to say, and you may not know what you need from them either.

You don't necessarily need someone to make you feel better. You may simply need someone who can sit with you.

You could tell someone:

"I don't need advice. I just don't want to be alone with this today."
"Can I tell you a story about them?"

Connection doesn't remove grief. But you don't have to carry it entirely on your own.

How can somatic
counselling support grief?

Grief isn't a problem that needs to be solved. The aim isn't to make you stop missing someone, erase memories or reach a predetermined state of "acceptance."

In somatic counselling, we can instead become curious about how the loss is being experienced in your body, your emotions and your everyday life.

You might notice a tightening in your chest when you talk about someone you've lost.

You might feel warmth when remembering a particular moment.

You might notice yourself becoming overwhelmed and wanting to disconnect.

Or you might not feel much in your body at all.

There is no right response.

The work can be slow and collaborative. Depending on what feels appropriate, a session may involve conversation, noticing bodily sensations, gentle movement, grounding or exploring a memory while paying attention to what happens in the body.

We don't need to force an emotional experience.

And we don't need to turn grief into something that needs to be "fixed."

Instead, we can create space to understand what you're experiencing and find ways of being with it that feel manageable.

If grief remains extremely intense or continues to significantly interfere with everyday life, it can also be important to seek specialised mental-health support. Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) is recognised in the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, and evidence-based treatments exist for it.

Somatic counselling is not a replacement for specialised treatment when a clinical disorder is present.

Grief doesn't have
a finish line

Perhaps one of the hardest things about grief is accepting that there may never be a moment when you suddenly feel completely "over it."

Your relationship changes.

Your life changes.

And over time, you may learn to carry the loss differently.

You might laugh again without feeling guilty. You might have days when you don't think about the person.

And then a song, a smell, a place or a particular date can bring everything back.

That doesn't necessarily mean you've gone backwards.

Grief isn't about forgetting someone. It's about learning to live in a world that has changed because they are no longer in it.

You don't have to figure that out all at once.

If you're navigating grief

You don't have to know exactly what you need before reaching out.

Sources & further reading

Kübler-Ross, E. & Kessler, D. — On Grief and Grieving.

Kübler-Ross and Kessler's work adapted the five-stage framework to bereavement. Kessler emphasises that the stages are not a linear timeline or a checklist, but a framework that can help people identify experiences that may arise in grief.

Stroebe, M. & Schut, H. — The Dual Process Model of Coping with Bereavement.

This influential model describes the movement between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping.

Hewson, H., Galbraith, N., Jones, C. & Heath, G. — The Impact of Continuing Bonds Following Bereavement: A Systematic Review.

A systematic review examining the different ways continuing bonds can relate to adjustment following bereavement.

Cunningham, J., Shevlin, M., Cerda, C. & McElroy, E. — ICD-11 Prolonged Grief Disorder, Physical Health, and Somatic Problems: A Systematic Review.

A systematic review examining associations between prolonged grief disorder and physical and somatic health outcomes.

Huberman Lab — The Science & Process of Healing from Grief.

Andrew Huberman's discussion of the neuroscience of grief, attachment, yearning and adapting our representations of relationships following loss.
