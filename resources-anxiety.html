WHEN ANXIETY
TAKES HOLD

Understanding anxiety, the brain, body and what can help

You can know you're safe and still feel like something is wrong.

You can tell yourself, "There is nothing to worry about," while your heart is racing, your chest feels tight and your mind is already imagining everything that could go wrong. Maybe you wake up tense before anything has even happened. Maybe you replay conversations, anticipate problems or constantly look for signs that something isn't quite right. Or perhaps you don't experience anxiety primarily as thoughts at all. Maybe you feel it in your stomach, your shoulders, your breathing or your inability to sit still.

Anxiety isn't just something that happens in your mind.

It involves your brain, your body, your behaviour, your memories and the way you have learned to respond to uncertainty and perceived threat. And understanding that can change the way you relate to it.

Anxiety is
not the enemy

Anxiety is an adaptive response.

Your brain is constantly making predictions about what might happen next and preparing you to respond to possible threats. When your brain detects danger, whether that danger is physical, social or psychological, your body can mobilise quickly.

  • Your heart rate can increase.
  • Your muscles can tense.
  • Your breathing can change.
  • Your attention can narrow towards whatever seems important.
  • Your digestive system can temporarily take a back seat.

None of this means that your body is broken.

In fact, the ability to become alert in response to potential danger is one of the things that keeps us alive.

The difficulty comes when that alarm becomes too sensitive, too frequent or too difficult to switch off. That distinction matters. Feeling anxious from time to time is a normal human experience. An anxiety disorder is different: anxiety or fear becomes excessive, persistent or difficult to control and begins to cause significant distress or interfere with everyday life.

What is actually
happening in your brain?

Your brain doesn't wait for danger to arrive before it reacts. It is constantly trying to predict what might happen next. To do this, it draws on information coming in from the world around you, your previous experiences, your memories and what you have learned to expect.

Several interconnected brain regions are involved in this process. Three that are particularly important when we talk about threat and anxiety are the amygdala, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex.

The amygdala: detecting what might matter

The amygdala is a small structure deep within the brain that plays an important role in detecting and learning about emotionally significant stimuli, particularly those associated with threat. You can think of it as part of the brain's threat-detection and learning system.

Importantly, the amygdala doesn't only respond to things that are objectively dangerous. Through learning, previously neutral cues can become associated with threat.

  • A particular place.
  • A tone of voice.
  • A physical sensation.
  • A social situation.
  • Even something that reminds you of a previous experience.

Once an association has been learned, encountering that cue can activate the threat response even when the original danger is no longer present.

The hippocampus: remembering the context

The hippocampus helps provide context. It is heavily involved in memory, including remembering where and when something happened.

This matters because the brain doesn't just need to learn:

"This is dangerous."

It also needs to learn:

"This was dangerous there, then, under those circumstances."

The hippocampus is part of the network that helps distinguish between different contexts and contributes to fear and safety learning. When this contextual learning doesn't work effectively, something that resembles a previous threat can sometimes trigger a response even when the current situation is actually safe.

The prefrontal cortex: putting things into perspective

Then there is the prefrontal cortex, a collection of regions involved in higher-order processes including attention, appraisal, decision-making and aspects of emotional regulation. It doesn't simply "turn off" the amygdala.

Instead, the prefrontal cortex is part of a broader network that helps evaluate what is happening, use context and regulate responses to emotionally significant information. This gives us a useful way of understanding something many people with anxiety experience:

You can know intellectually that you're safe while another part of your threat system is still responding as though something is wrong.

Your conscious mind might be saying:

"My manager hasn't actually said anything is wrong."

while your body is already preparing for the possibility that something is.

This isn't because your "emotional brain" has taken over and your rational brain has switched off. The reality is more interesting: multiple interconnected systems are processing threat, memory, context and regulation at the same time. This is one reason that simply telling yourself to "stop worrying" doesn't always make the anxiety disappear.

Knowing that you're safe and feeling safe are not always the same experience.

So where does
threat learning come in?

Imagine that you once experienced something frightening in a particular environment. Your brain learns an association between aspects of that experience and what happened. Over time, a cue that was previously neutral can begin to trigger a protective response because it predicts, or reminds you of, something threatening.

This is known as threat learning.

And importantly, learning can also go in the other direction.

"This is no longer dangerous."

This is known as safety learning and is a major area of anxiety research.

Modern research suggests that anxiety disorders can involve differences in threat acquisition, extinction and the ability to recall safety learning. This is one reason exposure-based treatments are so important: they can provide opportunities for new learning rather than simply trying to convince yourself that you are safe.

The original danger may be long gone. But the learned association can remain. And sometimes, the brain needs new experiences to update what it has learned.

Your body can respond to what something reminds you of, not only to what is actually happening now.

And this is where past experiences can matter.

Can trauma make you
more sensitive to anxiety?

For some people, anxiety begins or becomes much more intense after periods of chronic stress, adversity or trauma. Research supports an association between childhood trauma and later anxiety disorders, including generalized anxiety, social anxiety, panic and other anxiety-related conditions. But there is an important nuance here.

Not everyone who experiences trauma develops anxiety.

And not everyone who experiences anxiety has experienced trauma.

So I would be cautious about saying that trauma simply becomes "stuck in your nervous system." A more useful way of thinking about it is:

Sometimes your system learns something about what to expect.

If you have repeatedly experienced situations that felt unsafe, unpredictable or overwhelming, certain situations, sensations or relationships may become associated with threat. The original danger can be over, while the learned response remains sensitive. This doesn't mean there is necessarily one traumatic event you need to uncover.

It can simply be useful to ask:

What has my system learned to expect?

When anxiety becomes
a body experience

Anxiety isn't always experienced as the thought "I'm anxious."

Sometimes it feels like waking up already tense. A racing heart. A tight chest. A stomach that won't settle. Restlessness that makes sitting still difficult. A mind scanning for what could go wrong. Difficulty sleeping because your body doesn't seem to understand that the day is over.

You might notice:

  • racing or pounding heart
  • tightness in your chest
  • shallow or rapid breathing
  • muscle tension
  • stomach discomfort or nausea
  • sweating or trembling
  • restlessness
  • difficulty sleeping
  • difficulty concentrating
  • feeling constantly "on edge"

These physical experiences are a recognised part of anxiety. Anxiety disorders commonly involve physical tension alongside cognitive and behavioural symptoms.

But there is another important distinction:

A physical symptom does not automatically mean anxiety.

Chest pain, dizziness, shortness of breath and other physical symptoms can have many causes. New, severe or unexplained physical symptoms deserve appropriate medical assessment.

What does the science
actually suggest?

There isn't one single way of working with anxiety. Over decades of research, psychologists and researchers have developed different approaches that help us understand why anxiety persists and how people can learn to respond differently. Here are a few of the ideas I find most useful.

Understand the anxiety cycle

CBT: Aaron Beck, David Clark and others

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is one of the best-established psychological treatments for anxiety disorders. Research across anxiety-related disorders supports its effectiveness.

One useful CBT framework is the relationship between:

Situation → interpretation → body response → behaviour

For example:

Your manager sends: "Can we talk tomorrow?"

You interpret it as:

"I've done something wrong."

Your heart starts racing.

You reread the message several times and mentally rehearse the conversation.

You feel slightly better because you're doing something to manage the uncertainty.

But the cycle may also reinforce the idea that uncertainty is dangerous and that you need to check in order to feel safe.

You can try asking yourself:

  • What am I predicting?
  • What evidence do I actually have?
  • What am I doing right now to reduce uncertainty?
  • Is this helping me in the long term, or only making me feel better for the next five minutes?

I am not a clinical psychologist and I do not provide CBT. But understanding some of its principles can be useful psychoeducation and can help you recognise patterns in your own anxiety.

Move towards what you avoid

Exposure & inhibitory learning: Michelle Craske and colleagues

Avoidance makes sense. If something makes you anxious, staying away from it usually makes you feel better, at least temporarily. The problem is that avoidance can prevent you from discovering what would have happened if you had stayed. This is one reason exposure is an important component of evidence-based treatment for many anxiety disorders.

Modern exposure research, including Michelle Craske's work on inhibitory learning, has moved beyond the simple idea that exposure is about making fear disappear.

Instead, new learning can develop:

"I experienced this situation, and the catastrophe I predicted didn't happen."

Or:

"I felt anxious, and I was able to handle it."

Exposure is a structured therapeutic intervention, so this isn't a recommendation to overwhelm yourself or suddenly confront your biggest fear. For significant anxiety disorders, exposure should be undertaken with an appropriately qualified professional.

Sometimes the way forward is not avoiding the feeling, but gradually learning that you can experience it without letting it dictate everything you do.

Change your relationship with anxious thoughts

ACT: Steven Hayes and colleagues

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) offers another perspective.

Instead of making the goal:

"How do I get rid of this thought?"

ACT asks us to develop more psychological flexibility, the ability to experience difficult thoughts and feelings without automatically letting them determine our behaviour.

One simple ACT exercise is:

"I'm going to embarrass myself."

becomes:

"I'm having the thought that I'm going to embarrass myself."

The thought hasn't disappeared. You haven't convinced yourself that it's false.

You've simply created a little space between you and the thought.

Practise being where you actually are

Mindfulness & MBSR: Jon Kabat-Zinn

Mindfulness-based approaches offer another useful skill: noticing what is happening without immediately trying to solve or escape it. Jon Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) programme helped bring structured mindfulness practices into healthcare, and research has found benefits for anxiety symptoms, although the strength of evidence varies across populations and interventions.

Try this for a minute:

Notice.
What are you feeling?

Locate.
Where do you notice it in your body?

Name.
Can you describe the sensation without judging it?

Notice the story.
What is your mind predicting?

Return.
What is actually happening around you right now?

The goal isn't:

"I must make my anxiety disappear."

It is:

"Can I notice anxiety without immediately reacting to it?"

Learn to respond differently to difficult emotions

The Unified Protocol: David Barlow and colleagues

The Unified Protocol takes a transdiagnostic approach, focusing on emotional processes that occur across different anxiety and related disorders rather than treating every diagnosis as completely separate. One idea is particularly relevant:

The problem isn't necessarily that you experience anxiety. It can also be how you respond to experiencing anxiety.

  • Do you immediately suppress it?
  • Avoid it?
  • Seek reassurance?
  • Distract yourself?
  • Fight it?

Or can you learn to notice the emotion and choose your response more deliberately?

A practice I personally
return to

The Power of Now: Eckhart Tolle

One perspective that has been particularly meaningful to me is Eckhart Tolle's The Power of Now. I don't use Tolle's work as a scientific explanation for anxiety, and it isn't a substitute for evidence-based treatment.

What I appreciate is the simplicity of the question:

What is actually happening right now?

Anxiety often lives in the future.

What if this happens?

What if something goes wrong?

What if I can't cope?

Sometimes coming back to the present doesn't solve the problem. But it can help distinguish between what is happening now and what your mind is predicting might happen later.

For me, that distinction has been incredibly useful.

Try it yourself

The next time you notice your mind racing ahead, pause for a moment.

  • Feel your feet on the ground.
  • Look around you.
  • Notice your breathing without trying to change it.

And ask:

What is actually happening right now?

Not what might happen tomorrow.

Not what happened yesterday.

What is happening right now?

Where does somatic
counselling fit?

Much of the evidence-based work on anxiety focuses on thoughts, behaviours, avoidance, attention and emotion regulation. Somatic counselling adds another lens:

What happens when you pay attention to the physical side of the experience?

We might explore how anxiety shows up in your breathing, muscle tension, posture, movement or attention and how you respond when those sensations appear. This doesn't mean every physical sensation has a hidden meaning. And it doesn't mean we need to "release trauma from the body." It means learning to recognise your responses with more precision.

We can also explore the broader context: what tends to trigger your anxiety, what you do when it appears, whether certain patterns developed through prolonged stress or difficult experiences, and what helps you feel more capable of responding rather than simply reacting.

The aim isn't to make sure you never feel anxious again.

It's to help you develop more awareness and more choice in how you respond when anxiety appears.

A note about
anxiety disorders

Feeling anxious from time to time is a normal part of being human.

An anxiety disorder is different.

Anxiety disorders involve fear or anxiety that is excessive, persistent or difficult to control and causes significant distress or interferes with everyday life. They include conditions such as generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, agoraphobia and specific phobias.

If you think you may have an anxiety disorder, it is important to seek an assessment from an appropriately qualified healthcare or mental-health professional. Anxiety symptoms can also overlap with other physical and psychological conditions.

Evidence-based treatments are available, with psychological treatments based on CBT principles, including exposure, among the approaches with the strongest evidence across anxiety disorders.

Somatic counselling is not a substitute for diagnosis or clinical treatment of an anxiety disorder.

I am not a clinical psychologist and I do not provide CBT, psychotherapy or treatment for diagnosed anxiety disorders.

You don't have to
eliminate anxiety to live differently

Anxiety may always be part of being human. Sometimes it is a useful signal. Sometimes it is a learned response that has become overly sensitive. And sometimes it becomes severe enough to require professional treatment. The goal isn't necessarily to reach a life where you never feel anxious. It may be to recognise the feeling sooner. To understand what is happening. To know when to move towards something rather than avoid it. To notice what your body is doing without immediately believing that something is wrong. And, over time, to have more choice in what happens next.

Anxiety may be part of your experience. It doesn't have to run your life.

If you're navigating anxiety

You don't have to know exactly what you need before reaching out.

Somatic counselling can offer a space to slow down, talk about what you're experiencing and explore how anxiety is showing up in both your body and your life.

Sources & further reading

Beck, A. T. & colleagues — Cognitive Behavioural Therapy

CBT has one of the strongest evidence bases for anxiety disorders and has shaped much of our modern understanding of the relationship between thoughts, behaviours and emotional responses.

Craske, M. G. & colleagues — Inhibitory Learning and Exposure Therapy

Research exploring how exposure can create new learning and reduce avoidance, rather than relying solely on the disappearance of fear.

Hayes, S. C. & colleagues — Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

ACT focuses on psychological flexibility, acceptance, present-moment awareness and values-based action. Research supports its use for anxiety, while its evidence base continues to develop.

Kabat-Zinn, J. — Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction

MBSR introduced a structured mindfulness programme into healthcare and has been studied across a range of populations and conditions.

Barlow, D. H. & colleagues — The Unified Protocol

A transdiagnostic treatment approach targeting emotional processes shared across anxiety and related disorders.

World Health Organization — Anxiety Disorders

An overview of anxiety disorders, symptoms, contributing factors, evidence-based treatments and self-care strategies.
